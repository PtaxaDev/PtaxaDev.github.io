Back When I Could Still Walk

I was born in 2001 and currently live in the Dnipropetrovsk region. In 2016, I enrolled in college: I studied well, earned a stipend, and maintained great relationships with my teachers.


It was a wonderful time when I learned a lot and genuinely enjoyed studying. I also made two best friends who still visit me regularly. Overall, I lived a completely normal teenage life.

Hospital as a Second Home

In 2018, during my third year, I was struck by a gastrointestinal disease. At first, I was treated in my hometown hospital, but when doctors realized they couldn't cure me, they transferred me to a larger city hospital in Dnipro. They tried to fight for my health there as well, though they had an alternative option: performing a stoma procedure right away. While it would have caused some inconvenience, it wouldn't have led to the severe consequences I'm about to share.


A lot happened within those hospital walls, and everyone kept telling me they could cure me. Of course, that never happened. Whether they truly wanted to save me or simply claim bonuses for treating an unknown illness is something we'll never know.


So, I ended up in Kyiv—the capital, the hub of everything. Again, I was told I'd be cured (and they weren't lying about that part), but the cost turned out to be high. Due to the endless IV drips that accompanied me throughout the journey, I spent most of my time in bed—just watching drops fall from morning till night. There were brief moments without IVs, but they lasted only a second before a new stand with IV bags for the entire day was brought in. After prolonged treatment that yielded no results, my legs began to weaken, and gradually, the ability to walk slipped away. By the end of this wonderful "treatment," I couldn't stand up at all.


When "Day X" arrived, a surgeon came into my room (presumably required by protocol) and said:


"Look, we need to perform surgery, or you will die."


This happened a long time ago, so I can't guarantee every word is verbatim. Quite naturally, I agreed and went under the knife.


That's how I lost a part of myself. My legs? No, parts of my small and large intestines. Afterwards, as expected, I was sent to the ICU, where I spent a week before being transferred to a general ward. And honestly? I am immensely grateful to the surgeons and staff in Kyiv for saving my life.


I wish I could say everything went smoothly after that, but unfortunately not. While in the general ward, I had a panic attack (though I didn't realize it at the time), but it passed with my mother providing first aid. Sometime later, I was transferred back to Dnipro.


Once back, certain moments burned into my memory. Once, I needed an X-ray because my stoma had to be relocated due to bowel adhesions. I was brought into the X-ray room, but an unexpected problem arose: how do you take an X-ray on a post-Soviet machine if the patient can't stand? The staff's solution was to hold me up under my arms against the plate. Unsurprisingly, that was a terrible idea. As a result, I collapsed onto my knees on the floor (thankfully, it was wooden) and yelled through tears:


"Just put me on my fucking knees!"


And, lo and behold, it worked. I felt so sorry for my mom, who rushed into the room hearing my cries...


The main thing about the "return journey to Dnipro" is that I caught an infection in the hospital (most likely when my subclavian catheter was removed), and pure hell broke loose. Every single day, my temperature spiked to 39–40°C (102–104°F). Daily injections of analgin with diphenhydramine gave slight relief, just enough to sleep. I felt terrible up until the very last day of treatment.


Then came discharge day... I remember how happy I was when told it was my last IV drip. Those emotions are impossible to describe. I was discharged: unable to walk, with a stoma, barely alive.

Home, Sweet Home

After all those ordeals, it was comforting to finally be back home—after all, native walls help you heal. Unfortunately, my knee joints could no longer straighten by that time, but we still didn't lose hope.

It was tough at home: complete uncertainty, with no clear path forward or idea of how to live. Yet, we kept pushing through.

Sometime later, my mother fell severely ill. It is too painful and lengthy to detail, but she lost her mobility, and eventually her heart couldn't take it and stopped. I wouldn't wish anyone to see or go through that. Soon after, my paternal grandmother passed away as well.
